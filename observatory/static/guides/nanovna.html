NanoVNA-H4 · 10 kHz – 1.5 GHz · S11 / S21

Everything else your NanoVNA can do — and how.

You've made one measurement: an S11 SWR sweep of 902–928 MHz. That's genuinely the hardest first step — you already know what a sweep looks like and where the ports are. This guide is the rest of it: what the box is really measuring, why calibration is non-negotiable, and a catalog of hands-on jobs built around the antennas, pigtails, and coax in your Wildcat Mesh kit — plus the ham bands you'll be on once the Technician ticket comes through.

How to use this guide

Read sections 1–3 once (about 15 minutes). Then jump straight to Antenna SWR & tuning with a mesh antenna in hand, and come back to the other how-to cards as you need them. Section 8 is a concrete to-do list for this week. Menu names follow the DiSlord NanoVNA-D firmware most H4 units ship with; older/other firmware uses the same words in slightly different places.

01

What a VNA actually measures

A vector network analyzer is a tiny transmitter and two very precise receivers. It sends a known signal into whatever you connect to it, one frequency at a time across a sweep, and measures how much comes back and how much goes through — including phase, which is what "vector" means and what makes impedance and the Smith chart possible.

S11 — Reflection CH0 only

Signal goes out of CH0, hits your device (antenna, cable, filter input), and some fraction bounces back into CH0. S11 is that reflected fraction, with phase. From S11 alone the NanoVNA derives:

  • SWR — how badly the load mismatches 50 Ω (1.0 is perfect).
  • Return loss (LOGMAG, in dB) — same information, plotted as a dip; more negative = better match.
  • Impedance R + jX — resistance and reactance, shown on the Smith chart or as R / X / |Z| traces.
  • TDR — with a math transform, distance to cable faults and cable length.

This is what your first sweep was. One cable, one port, nothing connected to CH1.

S21 — Transmission CH0 → CH1

Signal leaves CH0, passes through your device, and is received at CH1. S21 is how much made it, with phase. It tells you:

  • Insertion loss / gain in dB — how much a cable, filter, attenuator, adapter chain, or amplifier eats (or adds).
  • Filter shape — passband, stopband, -3 dB bandwidth, roll-off.
  • Phase & group delay — mostly for filters and matching networks.
  • Series / shunt component values — the accurate way to measure small inductors and capacitors.

Two-port. You need a cable on each port and a THRU calibration.

The mental model

Everything RF is a question of whether energy goes where you want it. A 50 Ω system means every cable, connector, radio, and antenna is supposed to look like 50 Ω of resistance and no reactance. When something looks like 50 Ω, energy flows through it without bouncing. When it doesn't — an antenna cut for the wrong frequency, a corroded connector, a kinked cable — energy reflects. The VNA measures that reflection directly, and because it also measures phase it can tell you what kind of wrong the load is (too long, too short, too resistive) so you know which way to fix it.

Your instrument, specifically

Range

NanoVNA-H4: 10 kHz – 1.5 GHz. Native (fundamental) mode up to 300 MHz; 300–900 MHz and 900 MHz–1.5 GHz use harmonics of the internal synthesizer. Your 902–928 MHz band sits just inside the top region.

Dynamic range

Better than ~70 dB below 300 MHz, ~60 dB in 300–900 MHz, and ~40 dB in 900 MHz–1.5 GHz. At 915 MHz, 40 dB is still plenty for SWR/return-loss work (a −30 dB match is "perfect" for any practical purpose) — just don't expect to see the floor of a deep notch filter up there.

Resolution

Up to 401 sweep points on the H4 (the smaller H does 101). Narrow spans + more points = finer detail. The H4's 4-inch screen is what makes reading Smith charts on the device itself practical.

Output

About 0 dBm (1 mW). Harmless to any antenna. Whether the antenna is harmless to the VNA is section 7.

02

Calibration — the most important step

An uncalibrated NanoVNA measures its own cable, connectors, and internal bridge as much as your antenna. Calibration teaches it what "perfect open," "perfect short," and "perfect 50 Ω" look like at the exact spot you'll connect your device, so it can subtract everything before that spot. It takes ~90 seconds and it is the difference between numbers you can trust and numbers that look plausible.

Rule #1: recalibrate whenever you change the frequency range.

A calibration is only valid for the start/stop frequencies and point count it was made with. Change the sweep and the H4 will interpolate the old calibration (the status letter on the left drops to lowercase c) — usable in a pinch inside the original range, but wrong if you sweep outside it. Rule #2: also recalibrate if you change the cable/adapter chain, because the calibration plane moved.

The three (or five) standards

OPEN

Nothing connected (or the supplied OPEN cap). Teaches it what total reflection with 0° phase looks like.

S11 · required

SHORT

The shorted cap. Total reflection with 180° phase.

S11 · required

LOAD

The 50 Ω dummy load. "This is the target — no reflection at all." Sets the center of the Smith chart.

S11 · required

ISOLN

Isolation: LOAD on CH0, LOAD (or nothing) on CH1. Tells it how much leaks between ports internally. Optional; skip it unless you're measuring deep filter stopbands.

S21 · optional

THRU

Connect CH0's cable directly to CH1's cable (with a female-female barrel if both ends are male). "This is 0 dB loss."

S21 · required for any S21 work

The general flow

  1. Set the sweep first. STIMULUS → START / STOP (or CENTER / SPAN). For mesh work: 850–1000 MHz gives you context around the band; 902–928 MHz is a tight look. Pick a point count (SWEEP POINTS) now too — 201 or 401 for a tight span.
  2. Build the exact cable chain you'll measure through — e.g. the SMA cable plus the u.FL→SMA adapter if that's what the antenna needs. Calibration standards go on the far end of that chain. That end is your calibration plane; everything before it disappears from the measurement.
  3. CALIBRATE → RESET to clear any old calibration (so you can't accidentally blend two).
  4. CALIBRATE → CALIBRATE, then attach each standard and tap its button: OPEN → SHORT → LOAD. Each tap runs one sweep (the firmware temporarily narrows the IF bandwidth to 100 Hz for a cleaner reference). Let the trace settle before tapping. Watch the status column on the left: O, S, then D letters appear as each is collected.
  5. For S21: connect CH0 chain to CH1 chain and tap THRU. Optionally, with loads on both ports, tap ISOLN.
  6. Tap DONE. The firmware computes the error terms and shows C* (calibrated, unsaved) on the left. It then offers SAVE — pick a slot (the H4 has slots 0–6). Slot labels show their frequency range, so name-by-range is automatic.
  7. Sanity check: with the LOAD still on, the SWR trace should be a flat line at ~1.0 and the Smith marker should sit dead center. Put the OPEN on: the marker jumps to the far right edge. If not, something in the chain is loose.

Status letters, decoded

The narrow column on the left of the screen tells you the truth about your calibration:

C0…C6Calibration applied, from that saved slot. ✅
C*Calibrated but not saved to a slot (lost on power-off unless saved).
c0 / c*Lowercase = interpolated. You changed the sweep. Recalibrate if you care about the numbers.
(nothing)No calibration applied. Every number is suspect.
D R S T XError terms present: directivity, reflection tracking, source match, transmission tracking, isolation.
Pa / P2…P8Output drive. Turns red if it differs from what you calibrated with — recalibrate.

Things people get wrong

  • Calibrating at the VNA's SMA jack, then measuring through a cable. The cable's length shows up as phase rotation and its loss flattens your SWR. Calibrate at the cable's end.
  • Wide-span cal, narrow-span measurement. Interpolation between widely-spaced cal points is coarse. For the 902–928 band, calibrate over the band (or a modest margin around it).
  • Touching the standards while sweeping. Fingers on the OPEN change it. Set it down.
  • Finger-tight SMA. Snug is fine; over-torque strips brass adapters. Consistency matters more than force.
  • Forgetting the calibration kit's tolerance. The cheap included kit is fine to ~1 GHz for hobby work. The LOAD is the most important piece; if it ever gets damaged, replace it.
  • Blending calibrations. Hit RESET first. If the range or point count changed, the firmware clears the old set anyway when you collect a new standard — but be deliberate.
Suggested slot plan for your bench

C0 850–1000 MHz, 401 pts, cal at the SMA cable end (mesh antennas) · C1 902–928 MHz, 201 pts, same plane (fine tuning) · C2 1–500 MHz, 401 pts (general / TDR / ham VHF-UHF) · C3 140–150 MHz (2 m) · C4 420–450 MHz (70 cm) · C5 the same as C0 but with THRU done, for S21 pigtail/cable loss. Leave C6 as scratch. With CONFIG → EXPERT SETTINGS → REMEMBER STATE on, the H4 boots back into the last slot you used.

03

Reading the screen

Four traces, eight markers, a bottom line showing the sweep, and a status column. Learn to set a trace to what you want and put a marker where you care, and the rest is reading.

Setting the sweep

STIMULUS menu:

  • START / STOP — edges. The bottom line then shows START … STOP.
  • CENTER / SPAN — same thing, centered. Handy for "915 MHz ± 50".
  • CW FREQ — single frequency, no sweep. Great for live tuning: set CW to 915 MHz, watch SWR at the marker change as you trim.
  • SWEEP POINTS — 51 / 101 / 201 / 301 / 401. More points = slower sweep, finer detail.
  • Numeric keypad: type the number, then the unit key (M for MHz, G for GHz). BS backspace, Enter accept.
  • The lever (jog switch) nudges whichever value has the little arrow beside it; JOG STEP sets how far.

Traces & formats

DISPLAY → TRACE picks which of the four traces is active (yellow, cyan, green, magenta). Then FORMAT S11 or FORMAT S21 assigns what it shows:

  • LOGMAG — dB. For S11: return loss (dip = good). For S21: insertion loss / gain.
  • SWR — the antenna-tuner's view (S11 only). Dip = resonance.
  • SMITH — impedance, R + jX at the marker. Where the antenna is, not just how far off.
  • PHASE — degrees; crosses zero at resonance. Mostly for cable and filter work.
  • RESISTANCE / REACTANCE / |Z| — the Smith chart's numbers as flat plots.
  • SERIES L / SERIES C — component values straight from S11.
  • DELAY — group delay (filters).

A good default set: T1 S11 SWR T2 S11 SMITH T3 S11 LOGMAG T4 S21 LOGMAG

Markers

  • MARKER → SELECT MARKER → MARKER 1…8. The active one is what the top readout describes and what the lever moves.
  • SEARCH MINIMUM on an SWR trace jumps the marker to the resonance. TRACKING keeps it there sweep after sweep — turn this on while trimming an antenna.
  • DELTA shows other markers relative to the active one — e.g. marker 1 on the SWR minimum, markers 2 and 3 at the 2:1 SWR edges → instant bandwidth.
  • OPERATIONS → → CENTER / → SPAN re-center the sweep on the marker(s). Zoom in on a resonance in two taps (then recalibrate!).
  • Tap a marker on the touchscreen and drag it. Tap the top readout to change the active trace.

Scale & cleanliness

  • DISPLAY → SCALE → AUTO SCALE fits the active trace. SCALE/DIV, TOP, BOTTOM, REFERENCE POSITION for manual.
  • SWR trace: set scale 1/div with reference at the bottom so 1.0 is the baseline. 1.5 and 2.0 then read as grid lines.
  • IF BANDWIDTH (4 kHz → 30 Hz): narrower = less noise, slower sweep. Use 1 kHz or 333 Hz above 900 MHz where the noise floor is higher.
  • DATA SMOOTH averages neighboring points. Helps a jittery 915 MHz trace look honest; don't use it to hide a real problem.
  • STORE TRACE freezes a copy (red). Store "before," then trim, and compare "after" live. This is the trimming workflow.

Antenna SWR & resonance tuning — your 915 MHz LoRa antennas

S11Wildcat MeshCH0

What it's for. Answering the single most useful question in your mesh: is this antenna actually resonant where my radios transmit? A stock "915 MHz" whip that came in a bag is often tuned for 868 MHz (the EU LoRa band), or nowhere in particular. A mismatched antenna reflects power back into the SX1262 in your Heltec / E22 modules, cuts range, and over time can stress the PA. Ten minutes with the VNA tells you which antennas to keep.

Setup

  • SMA cable from CH0 → (adapter if needed) → antenna. Most mesh whips are SMA male; some are RP-SMA (the "reverse polarity" Wi-Fi style — pin in the wrong gender). Check before forcing anything. Keep an SMA↔RP-SMA adapter in the kit.
  • Calibrate at the end of the cable/adapter chain (slot C0: 850–1000 MHz, 401 pts).
  • Traces: T1 S11 SWR, T2 S11 SMITH. Marker 1 with SEARCH MINIMUM + TRACKING.

Steps

  1. Hold the antenna as it will be used, away from your hand, your body, the desk, and the VNA itself. A whip on a bare SMA jack has almost no ground plane; screw it onto the node's pigtail with the node powered off, or onto a small metal plate or the enclosure it will live in, for a realistic counterpoise. Your hand near a 915 MHz whip will move the dip by tens of MHz — that's not a bug, that's physics, and it's why handheld nodes vary.
  2. Read the SWR minimum frequency and value at M1. Note the SWR at 902, 915, and 928 (drop markers 2–4 there, or just read the trace).
  3. Decide: dip inside 902–928 and SWR < 2 across the band → keep, done. Dip below ~890 → antenna is electrically too long → trim (next step). Dip above ~940 → too short → it's not a 915 antenna; retire it to the parts bin.
  4. Trimming (only for whips you can cut — the rubber-duck / helical ones aren't trimmable): STORE TRACE first. Remove the tip cap, cut 1 mm at a time, re-measure. Near 915 MHz, 1 mm on a quarter-wave whip moves resonance about 10 MHz. It only goes one way; stop early.
  5. Check the Smith trace: at resonance the marker crosses the horizontal center line (reactance = 0). If it crosses left of center the antenna's resistance is below 50 Ω (common for short whips on small ground planes); right of center, above. You can't easily fix that without matching — but a 1.5 SWR from a 35 Ω resonant antenna is still a fine antenna.
  6. Save the slot; write the result on tape on the antenna (e.g. 915 ✓ 1.3). Future-you will thank you.

How to read it

  • SWR at 915 < 1.5 — excellent, ship it.
  • 1.5 – 2.0 across the band — normal for a compact whip. Fine.
  • > 3 anywhere in band — wrong antenna or broken connection. Check the pigtail first (see connectors).
  • No dip at all / flat SWR ~1 — you're measuring a dummy load or a lossy cable, not an antenna. Or the calibration isn't applied.
  • Dip jumps when you touch the cable — common-mode current on the coax; the antenna is using the cable as its ground plane. Realistic for tiny nodes.

Where Meshtastic actually transmits

The US default (LongFast, slot 20) is 906.875 MHz; other presets land elsewhere in 902–928. So the whole band matters, but if you're choosing between two antennas, prefer the one that's best near 907.

Numbers for 915 MHz

λ = 32.8 cm · λ/4 = 8.2 cm free-space
Practical λ/4 whip ≈ 7.6–7.9 cm of radiator (wire is electrically "fatter")
λ/2 ≈ 15.7 cm free-space
Trim sensitivity ≈ −10 MHz per mm cut (λ/4 whip)

Gotcha

A dip on the VNA proves the antenna is matched, not that it radiates well. A dummy load has a perfect dip. Match first, then confirm with real RSSI/SNR between two nodes.

Measuring coax / cable loss

S21S11 alt.Wildcat MeshCH0 → CH1

What it's for. Every dB lost in the feedline is a dB you don't get on the air — and at 915 MHz, thin coax is hungry (RG-174: roughly 0.5 dB per foot; RG-58: ~0.2 dB/ft; LMR-400: ~0.04 dB/ft). A 10 ft RG-58 run to a rooftop mesh node quietly throws away a third of your power. Measure it, then decide whether the run is worth upgrading.

Setup — S21 (the right way)

  • Short, good cable on CH0 and another on CH1. Calibrate including THRU with those two joined by a barrel (slot C5). The barrel is now part of the "zero."
  • Trace: S21 LOGMAG, scale 1 dB/div, reference at the top.

Steps

  1. Insert the cable under test between the two calibration cables (replacing the barrel, or with the barrel still in on one side — be consistent with the cal).
  2. Read S21 at your frequency. It's negative dB; the magnitude is the one-way loss. −1.2 dB at 915 MHz = 1.2 dB loss = 24 % of power gone.
  3. Sweep wide (1–1000 MHz) to see loss vs frequency — it should be a smooth curve growing with frequency. Bumps or ripples mean a bad connector or a damaged section.

S11 alternative (one port, no THRU)

Leave the far end of the cable open (or shorted) and look at S11 LOGMAG. The signal goes down and back, so the return loss you read is twice the cable loss. −3 dB S11 with an open far end = 1.5 dB cable loss. The DiSlord firmware automates this: MEASURE → CABLE (S11) reports loss and Att (dB/100m). Less accurate than S21 but good enough to sort cables into keep/toss.

How to read it

  • Matches the datasheet ± 20 % — healthy cable.
  • Much worse than datasheet — water ingress, crushed dielectric, or a bad crimp. The TDR card finds where.
  • Ripple in the S21 trace — an impedance bump reflecting at both ends. Bad connector.
  • Loss changes when you flex it — intermittent connection. Toss it.

Rule of thumb @ 915 MHz

CabledB / 10 ft
RG-174 / 1.13 mm pigtail≈ 4–5
RG-316≈ 3
RG-58≈ 2
RG-8X / LMR-240≈ 1.2 / 0.8
LMR-400≈ 0.4

Approximate; the point is that at 915 MHz, keep pigtails short and put the node at the antenna, not the antenna at the node.

Cable length & fault finding (TDR / distance-to-fault)

S11TRANSFORMCH0

What it's for. Time-domain reflectometry turns a frequency sweep into a plot of reflections vs distance along a cable. Every impedance change — a connector, a kink, a crushed spot, the end — shows as a bump at its distance. Measure a mystery cable's length, find where the rooftop run got pinched, or confirm a pigtail's electrical length. The NanoVNA computes this with an inverse FFT of S11 (the feature cho45 added to the original firmware).

Setup

  • Cable on CH0, calibrated at its end (that's distance zero). Connect the cable under test there; leave its far end open or shorted.
  • Sweep wide from low: e.g. 1–500 MHz or 50 kHz–900 MHz with 401 points. Span sets resolution; point spacing sets max distance: max distance ≈ (points−1) × VF × c ÷ (2 × span) — 401 pts over 500 MHz with VF 0.66 → ~80 m. Fine for anything in your house.
  • DISPLAY → TRANSFORM → VELOCITY F. = your cable's VF as a percent (RG-58/RG-174/RG-316 ≈ 66; RG-8X ≈ 82; LMR-400 ≈ 85; foam RG-6 ≈ 82). Wrong VF = right shape, wrong ruler.
  • TRANSFORM ON, LOW PASS STEP (shows impedance steps — the intuitive one) or LOW PASS IMPULSE (sharp spikes at each discontinuity). Trace S11 REAL or LINEAR.

Steps

  1. With the far end open, drop a marker on the big step/spike — that's the end. The marker readout shows time and distance (one-way, already halved).
  2. Any bump before the end is a discontinuity. Its distance is where to look for the fault; its polarity tells you the flavor (step up = higher impedance/open-ish, step down = lower/short-ish).
  3. Unknown cable type? Measure a known length and adjust VF until the end lands at the right distance — you've now measured VF. MEASURE → CABLE (S11) does this too: enter CABLE LENGTH and it reports VF and Z0.

How to read it

  • One clean step at the end, flat before it — good cable.
  • Small blip at 0.5 m then flat — that's your adapter/connector. Normal if small.
  • Big bump mid-run — damage there. Distance = position.
  • End appears at half the distance — VF is wrong, or the cable is actually shorter than the label.
  • Everything smeared — span too narrow. Widen the sweep, recalibrate.

NanoVNA-Saver's TDR tab is easier to read than the on-device one and lets you pick the cable type from a list.

Filter / duplexer / band-pass characterization

S21S11CH0 → CH1

What it's for. Seeing exactly what a filter passes and blocks. Relevant to you sooner than you'd think: a cheap 915 MHz SAW band-pass filter in front of a mesh node's receiver can rescue a site that's swamped by a nearby cell tower or pager transmitter; a "70 cm" duplexer for a repeater; a low-pass filter on an HF transmitter. The VNA shows the real passband, real insertion loss, and real stopband — not the datasheet's.

Setup

  • CH0 → filter input, filter output → CH1. Two-port cal including THRU (and ISOLN if you care about deep stopbands).
  • Sweep wide enough to see the skirts: for a 915 MHz SAW, try 700–1100 MHz. Traces: S21 LOGMAG (10 dB/div, reference at top) and S11 LOGMAG (input match).

Steps

  1. Marker on the S21 peak: that's the insertion loss (a good SAW: −2 to −3 dB; an LC band-pass: −0.5 to −1.5 dB).
  2. MEASURE → FILTER (S21): the firmware finds the peak and the −3 dB and −6 dB edges and prints center frequency, bandwidth, Q, and roll-off in dB/octave. Otherwise use DELTA markers 3 dB down on each side.
  3. Check that your frequencies are inside the flat top with margin — a 915 filter whose −3 dB edge is at 905 MHz will hurt LongFast at 906.875.
  4. Look at the stopband depth at the interferer's frequency (e.g. 850 MHz cellular). Remember the H4's ~40 dB dynamic range above 900 MHz — a −60 dB stopband will read as −40. That's the instrument floor, not the filter.
  5. Swap the ports (or just look at S11) to confirm the input is matched in the passband; a filter that passes but reflects is still a loss.

How to read it

  • Flat top, steep sides, low loss — good filter.
  • Passband tilted or rippled — mismatch; check cal and connectors.
  • Peak shifted from the label — cheap SAWs drift with temperature and lot; that's why you measure.
  • Stopband "floor" around −40 dB above 900 MHz — instrument limit, not the filter.

Duplexers

Each pass leg is a filter: measure ANT→RX (should pass the RX freq, notch TX) and ANT→TX (the reverse). Tuning cans is the classic "watch the notch move on S21 while turning the screw" job — set CW at the notch frequency for live feedback, or use marker tracking on SEARCH MINIMUM.

Impedance matching & how to read the Smith chart

S11SMITHCH0

What it's for. SWR tells you how far from 50 Ω. The Smith chart tells you which direction — and therefore what to do about it. It's just a map of every possible impedance, bent so that 50 Ω is the bull's-eye. Once it clicks, you'll never want a plain SWR trace again.

Steps

  1. Trace S11 SMITH. Tap SMITH again to choose the marker readout: R + jX for raw impedance, or R + L/C to see the reactance as the equivalent inductance or capacitance (the value you'd need to cancel it).
  2. Put the marker at your operating frequency. Read R and X.
  3. Diagnose: X positive → inductive → the load is "long"; shorten it or add series C. X negative → capacitive → "short"; lengthen it or add series L. R off 50 with X ≈ 0 → resonant but wrong resistance; needs a transformer or an L-network.
  4. MEASURE → L/C MATCH prints the L-network (series + shunt component values) that would match the marker impedance to 50 Ω. Real components at 915 MHz are tiny (a few nH / pF) — surface-mount territory, but it tells you what the antenna designer was up against.

Cheat sheet

  • Marker at center → 50 Ω, matched.
  • On the line, left of center → resonant, R < 50 (typical small whip: 25–40 Ω).
  • On the line, right → resonant, R > 50 (folded dipoles, some end-feds).
  • Upper half → inductive. Shorten.
  • Lower half → capacitive. Lengthen.
  • Hugging the outer rim → total reflection: open, short, or a nonresonant stub. Almost no power accepted.
  • Trace spirals in tight loops → you're looking through a long cable; each loop is a half-wave of cable. Calibrate at the cable end to un-spin it (or set E-DELAY).

Why a trace goes clockwise

Phase delay grows with frequency, so the reflection's phase rotates one way. Direction is a sanity check: a counter-clockwise trace means your start/stop are swapped or the data is being read backwards.

The admittance grid

Choosing a G + jB readout flips the chart to its mirror (admittance) — the right view for shunt components. Don't panic when the grid changes; tap SMITH → R + jX to get the familiar one back.

Checking baluns, ununs & common-mode chokes

S21S11CH0 → CH1

What it's for. When you build a wire antenna for HF or a dipole for 2 m / 70 cm, the balun or choke at the feedpoint decides whether the coax shield becomes part of the antenna (RF in the shack, noisy receive, wandering SWR). Three questions: does it pass signal with low loss, does it transform impedance correctly, and does it actually block common-mode current at the bands you use?

A · Insertion loss and match (any balun/unun)

  1. Two-port cal with THRU. CH0 → balun input; balun output → CH1. Trace S21 LOGMAG, sweep the band(s) of interest.
  2. For a 1:1 the loss should be well under 0.5 dB across the band. For 4:1 or 9:1, S21 alone is misleading (CH1 is 50 Ω, not 200/450) — so instead:
  3. Put the correct resistor on the output (200 Ω for a 4:1, 450 for a 9:1 — a non-inductive resistor soldered to a connector) and read S11 SWR at the input. Flat and low across the band = the transformer is doing its job.
  4. Back-to-back trick: two identical baluns output-to-output, S21 through the pair; half the total loss is each one's.

B · Common-mode choking impedance (the one that matters)

  1. Treat the choke as a series device: CH0 center pin → one end of the choke's winding (shield and center tied together, i.e. the "common-mode" path) → other end → CH1 center pin. Ground the VNA side of both.
  2. Trace S21, format SERIES |Z| (the firmware converts the attenuation into the series impedance that caused it). Or read S21 LOGMAG and use the rule: −20 dB ≈ 1 kΩ, −26 dB ≈ 2 kΩ, −32 dB ≈ 4 kΩ.
  3. Sweep 1–60 MHz for HF chokes, 100–500 MHz for VHF/UHF ones. You want > 1 kΩ, ideally 3–5 kΩ, across the bands you operate — and mostly resistive (check the phase or SERIES R vs X), which is what makes ferrite chokes better than air-core coils.

How to read it

  • S21 within −0.3 dB, S11 SWR < 1.3 with the right load — transformer is good.
  • Choke |Z| peaks at 5 kΩ on 40 m but 300 Ω on 10 m — normal: chokes are band-specific. Choose ferrite mix by band (Mix 31 for low HF, 43 for upper HF, 61 for VHF).
  • Loss rising steeply at the top of the band — the core is saturating/self-resonating; too many turns for that frequency.
  • Great choke on the bench, still RF in the shack — the antenna is unbalanced for a different reason (asymmetric legs, coax running parallel to a leg). The VNA can only test the part.

Ununs for your future end-fed

A 49:1 EFHW transformer wants ~2450 Ω on its output for the S11 test. Two 4.7 kΩ resistors in parallel works. Look for SWR < 1.5 on the bands you care about and watch loss — a bad 49:1 turns your RF into core heat.

Measuring inductors & capacitors, and an LC's resonant frequency

S11S21CH0 (+CH1)

What it's for. Mystery parts from the junk drawer, homemade coils for a matching network or a trap, checking a capacitor's real value at RF (they drift), and finding where an LC actually resonates before you build a filter around it. Also the fastest way to learn what "reactance" feels like: watch the Smith marker slide around the rim as you change frequency.

Method 1 · S11 direct read (quick; best for 10 nH–10 µH, 5 pF–10 nF)

  1. Calibrate at a female SMA jack (or a small test fixture: an SMA connector with two short pads). Solder or clip the part between center and ground, leads as short as possible.
  2. Trace S11 format SERIES L (for a coil) or SERIES C (for a cap). Sweep a range where the part's reactance is between about 10 Ω and 500 Ω — the VNA is most accurate there. For a 100 nH coil that's roughly 20–500 MHz; for a 100 pF cap, 3–150 MHz.
  3. Read the value at a marker. Ignore any frequency where the reading flips sign or dives to zero — that's the part's self-resonance, above which a coil looks like a cap and vice versa.

Method 2 · S21 shunt / series (accurate for very small or very large values)

  1. Two-port cal with THRU. Put the part either in series between CH0 and CH1 (good for big |Z|: large L, small C) or in shunt from the through line to ground (good for small |Z|: small L, large C).
  2. Trace S21 format SERIES |Z| / SERIES X or SHUNT |Z| / SHUNT X. The firmware reports the component's reactance and the Smith readout (R+L/C SER..) gives L or C directly.

Method 3 · LC resonance

  1. Series LC (coil and cap in series) across CH0: S11 shows a sharp dip toward the short point at resonance. Or use MEASURE → RESONANCE (S11), which lists every frequency where reactance crosses zero.
  2. For a parallel LC "tank" (traps, tuned circuits): loosely couple it in shunt or series in a two-port line and use MEASURE → SHUNT LC / SERIES LC (S21). It finds the resonance and prints L, C, R, and Q.

How to read it

  • Flat value across a decade of frequency — clean part, trust it.
  • Value rising with frequency, then blowing up — approaching self-resonance. Use the low-frequency value.
  • Different value than the label — normal for ±20 % ceramic caps and hand-wound coils. Now you know.
  • Reading jumps when you touch the fixture — stray capacitance; at 915 MHz, 1 pF is 174 Ω. Keep hands off, leads short.

Checking connectors, adapters & pigtails (your u.FL → SMA leads)

S21S11Wildcat Mesh

What it's for. The u.FL (IPEX / MHF) connector on a Heltec board and an E22 module is rated for about 30 mating cycles and is easy to half-seat. The tiny 1.13 mm pigtail behind it is lossy at 915 MHz and easy to crimp. A bad pigtail looks exactly like a bad antenna on an SWR sweep — so test the pigtail alone, first, and you'll never chase the wrong problem.

Setup

  • You need a u.FL↔SMA adapter or a second known-good pigtail to get from the u.FL end to the VNA's SMA. (A short u.FL-female-to-SMA-female adapter lead is worth owning; note it in the calibration.)
  • Two-port cal with THRU (slot C5), 850–1000 MHz. If you can only do one port, the S11 method below still catches gross faults.

Steps — S21 loss (the definitive test)

  1. CH0 cable → pigtail SMA end. Pigtail u.FL end → u.FL adapter → CH1 cable. S21 LOGMAG, 0.5 dB/div.
  2. A 10 cm 1.13 mm pigtail should read about −0.3 to −0.6 dB at 915 MHz, plus a little for the adapter. Longer pigtails scale: ~1.5 dB per 30 cm for 1.13 mm, ~1 dB per 30 cm for RG-178/316.
  3. Wiggle test: flex the pigtail and rotate the u.FL gently while watching S21. Any jump = intermittent. Retire it.
  4. Rank your pigtails by loss. Label the best ones for the nodes that matter (the solar repeater), use the worst for bench testing only.

Steps — S11 quick check (one port)

  1. Calibrate at CH0's cable end. Attach the pigtail with the 50 Ω LOAD on its far end (via the u.FL adapter). S11 LOGMAG should be below about −20 dB across the band (SWR < 1.2). Anything worse than −15 dB is a suspect connector.
  2. Far end open: S11 near 0 dB minus twice the pigtail loss (−1 dB → 0.5 dB loss). A reading like −6 dB with the far end open means the pigtail is eating 3 dB — broken shield or water.

How to read it

  • −0.3 to −0.8 dB, stable when flexed — good pigtail.
  • > 1.5 dB for a short lead — damaged. Or it's RG-174 and 30 cm long; both are reasons to replace it.
  • Reading changes with u.FL rotation — the u.FL is worn or not fully clicked. Reseat straight down until it snaps; if it still moves, replace the pigtail.
  • SMA vs RP-SMA confusion — an RP-SMA-female adapter on an SMA-male antenna "screws on" but the pin doesn't mate: S11 shows open (rim of the Smith chart). Now you know why the node has no range.

Adapters & barrels

Test adapters by putting them in the THRU path and reading S21: a good SMA barrel or SMA↔N adapter costs < 0.1 dB at 915 MHz. Cheap ones with mis-sized pins can cost 0.5 dB and show as a ripple on a wide sweep. Bin the bad ones.

u.FL care

Press straight down, never at an angle; lift with a proper u.FL puller (or a fingernail under the metal skirt, straight up). Never pull by the cable. If a node has a switchable "antenna select" resistor (some Heltec boards do), confirm which path is active before blaming the connector.

Ham antenna tuning across the bands you'll use

S11TechnicianCH0

What it's for. The same SWR-dip workflow as the mesh antennas, at bigger wavelengths. As a Technician you get all of 2 m (144–148), 70 cm (420–450), 1.25 m (222–225), 6 m (50–54), plus 10 m SSB/CW (28.0–28.5 MHz) and CW slices of 80/40/15 m. The NanoVNA covers all of it in its cleanest (fundamental, < 300 MHz) range except 70 cm, which is still comfortably inside the 60 dB region.

Setup

  • Calibrate per band at the end of the coax you'll actually use (or at the antenna feedpoint, then measure again through the coax to see what the radio sees — the difference is the feedline's loss flattening the SWR).
  • Slots: C3 = 140–150 MHz, C4 = 420–450 MHz, plus a wide 1–500 MHz one to find where a mystery antenna lives.
  • Traces: S11 SWR + SMITH. Markers at band edges and your favorite frequency (e.g. 146.52 simplex, your local repeater).

Steps

  1. Wide sweep first (1–500 MHz) with a dual-band HT antenna: you'll see two dips, ~146 and ~440. A "tri-band" adds ~222. Anything else the seller claimed — you'll see if it's real.
  2. Narrow to the band. Check SWR at the band edges and center. HT rubber ducks are typically 1.5–3 on the VNA without a body nearby — they're designed to use you as the counterpoise, so hold the HT-shaped ground (or clip a 19 in. counterpoise wire "tiger tail" to the SMA shell and watch the SWR drop; that's a real, measurable upgrade).
  3. Dipole / J-pole / ground plane you build: cut long (formula length + 5 %), measure, trim both legs equally. For 2 m, ~1 cm per leg moves resonance ~1 MHz; for 70 cm, ~3 mm per ~1 MHz. Store the "before" trace and watch it slide up.
  4. Mobile / base antennas: measure through the feedline from the shack end. SWR that looks great at the radio but was mediocre at the feedpoint = lossy coax hiding a bad match. Go fix the antenna anyway.
  5. Record final SWR at band edges in your log. Repeat seasonally — antennas move with weather, corrosion, and tree growth.

How to read it

  • 2 m ≤ 1.5 across 144–148 — done.
  • Dip at 143, 2.5 at 148 — slightly long. Trim.
  • 70 cm dip at 435 but 3+ at 446 — narrow-band antenna; fine for one repeater, not for the whole band.
  • SWR bottoms at 1.8 no matter what — resistance isn't 50 Ω at resonance. Adjust the antenna's geometry (J-pole tap point, ground-plane radial droop ~45°) rather than length.

Saving & recalling calibrations and setups

A saved slot is more than the calibration: it stores the sweep range, point count, traces, formats, markers, and electrical delay together. Recalling a slot brings your whole bench setup back.

Internal slots

  • CALIBRATE → SAVE → 0…6 (7 slots on the H4). A populated slot shows its start/stop frequency as its label; empty ones say Empty N. Saving overwrites without asking.
  • RECALL → slot. The status column switches to C + that slot number.
  • Slot 0 is what the H4 loads at power-on (unless REMEMBER STATE is on, in which case it restores whatever you used last). Put your everyday setup — say the 850–1000 MHz mesh cal — in slot 0.
  • Slot contents survive power-off and battery drain; they don't survive a firmware update or CLEAR CONFIG. Re-do them after flashing.
  • CALIBRATE → RANGE shows a slot's point count and frequency range, and snaps the sweep back to it if you've drifted (undoing the lowercase-c interpolation).

SD card (H4 has a microSD slot)

  • SD CARD → SAVE CALIBRATION writes a .cal file: the complete slot contents. LOAD → LOAD CAL brings it back. Unlimited "slots," and they survive firmware flashes (as long as the firmware version's file layout matches — re-save after major updates).
  • SAVE S1P / SAVE S2P writes the current sweep as a Touchstone file — the standard interchange format. Open it later in NanoVNA-Saver, or keep it as a permanent record of "the T096 antenna on 2026-09-21."
  • SCREENSHOT saves a BMP of the screen. Great for a notebook or a forum post.
  • AUTO NAME on = files named by date/time from the built-in clock (set it under CONFIG → DATE/TIME). Off = you type a name.
  • Card format: FAT32, files in the root directory. A small (≤ 32 GB) card is safest.
Device config vs. slot properties

Touch calibration, brightness, IF bandwidth default, and the like are device config: save them with CONFIG → SAVE CONFIG, which is separate from calibration slots. If you re-do the touch calibration (CONFIG → TOUCH CAL, tap the two corner targets) remember to SAVE CONFIG afterward or it's gone at reboot.

06

Connecting to a computer: NanoVNA-Saver

The H4 is a USB serial device. Plug it into a Mac/PC/Linux box and free software drives it: bigger charts, more sweep points than the screen can show, exports, and a friendlier calibration and TDR interface. NanoVNA-Saver is the community standard.

What it adds

  • A real screen. Multiple charts at once: SWR, Smith, S11/S21 logmag, phase, group delay, R/X, TDR, all resizable.
  • Segmented sweeps. It sweeps a wide span in slices and stitches them — thousands of points instead of 401. A 1–1000 MHz "what is this antenna" sweep becomes actually readable.
  • Averaging across repeated sweeps for a quieter trace at 915 MHz.
  • Calibration in software, with optional correction for the standards' real (non-ideal) values, save/load with notes. Its cal can be more precise than the device's, especially for S21.
  • Markers with band tables, min-SWR finder, bandwidth readouts, and a "band" overlay for the ham allocations.
  • Touchstone export/import (.s1p/.s2p), CSV, and chart image export. Load two files and compare before/after.
  • TDR panel with cable-type presets (VF built in) and a distance axis you can actually read.

Getting connected

  1. Install from github.com/NanoVNA-Saver/nanovna-saver — prebuilt macOS/Windows/Linux releases, or pip install nanovna-saver in a venv.
  2. USB-C the H4 to the computer. On macOS/Linux no driver is needed (it's a CDC serial device); Windows 10+ also usually just works. The H4 must be on and on its normal screen (not DFU).
  3. In NanoVNA-Saver, pick the serial port (it auto-detects /dev/cu.usbmodem… on a Mac), click Connect.
  4. Set start/stop, segments (e.g. 5 segments × 401 = 2005 points), click Sweep.
  5. Calibrate in the app (Calibration → Calibration assistant walks you through OPEN/SHORT/LOAD/ISOLN/THRU) — this cal lives on the computer, independent of the device's slots. Keep the device slot cal off or make them agree; don't stack them unknowingly.

The upstream ttrftech repo also lists NanoVNA-App (Windows), cho45's WebUSB client (runs in Chrome, zero install), and a Python/Jupyter interface if you ever want to script sweeps of a dozen antennas.

When to bother

  • On the bench: always. The screen is worth it and you get a saved record.
  • On the roof / in the field: the H4 alone. That's why you bought the 4-inch one.
  • Documenting an antenna build or comparing two antennas: export .s1p from both, overlay them.
  • Firmware updates (DiSlord's NanoVNA-D, releases): CONFIG → EXPERT SETTINGS → DFU, then flash with dfu-util or the STM32 tool. Re-do your slot calibrations after. Don't chase every release; update when a feature you want lands.
07

Safety & limits — how not to kill it

The NanoVNA's front end is a resistive bridge feeding a sensitive mixer with no input protection to speak of. It's a receiver, not a radio. The failures below are the ones that actually happen to people.

Never connect…

  • A transmitting radio. Not a Meshtastic node that's powered on (SX1262 modules put out +20 to +22 dBm — 100+ mW), not an HT, not an E22 with power applied. The ports are measurement inputs. Power the node off and unplug its antenna lead before the VNA touches it. Keying a 5 W HT into CH0 will destroy the bridge and the mixer instantly.
  • DC voltage. Bias-tees, phantom-powered LNAs, active antennas, anything with a supply on the coax. The safe working assumption is no DC at all; the widely-quoted ceiling is about 10 V, but nothing good happens above zero.
  • A big antenna during a storm, or a long unterminated coax you just dragged across carpet. Static.
  • An antenna next to a strong transmitter. A 915 MHz antenna a few meters from a cell site, or a 2 m antenna beside a repeater, picks up enough to distort readings — and, in extreme cases, damage the input.

The numbers

  • Treat +10 dBm (10 mW) as the absolute max on either port. Some designers quote higher bridge tolerances (up to ~+20 dBm on some models), but the H4 has no documented margin above that and the mixer is the weak link. +10 dBm is the conservative community figure — stay far below it.
  • ~0 dBm output from CH0. It cannot hurt anything you connect to it, including a powered-off radio's antenna port.
  • Any external DC: avoid. Use a DC block (a series capacitor in an SMA barrel) if you must measure something with a supply present.
  • Lithium cell: charge over USB-C at ≤ 2 A; the H4's battery icon turns red below ~3.3 V. Don't store it fully dead for months.

ESD habits

  • Touch the VNA's SMA shell or a grounded metal object before connecting a cable, especially in dry weather.
  • Discharge a long coax before connecting it: short the center pin to the shell with a screwdriver, or momentarily connect a 50 Ω load. Coax is a capacitor (~100 pF/m) and a whip antenna in wind is a static generator.
  • Keep the calibration standards in their bag. The LOAD is the crown jewel.
  • Put dust caps on CH0/CH1 when not in use; a bent center pin is the second most common cause of "my NanoVNA reads garbage."

Connector habits

  • Finger-tight SMA. Turn the nut, not the cable or the adapter body, so the center pin doesn't twist.
  • Use an SMA "saver" — a cheap male-female adapter left permanently on each port. Wear the saver out, not the H4's soldered jacks.
  • Never mate SMA with RP-SMA or force N onto anything. If it takes effort, stop.
  • Don't let cables dangle off the ports; the jacks are board-mounted.

Interpreting weird readings

  • SWR trace jumps around at 915 MHz — normal noise in the top harmonic region; narrow the IF bandwidth, add smoothing, or use NanoVNA-Saver averaging.
  • Everything reads open, even the LOAD — bent/pushed-in center pin, or cal not applied. Check for the C.
  • OPEN and SHORT look the same — the bridge is damaged (see: transmitting radio, above). Time for a new one; they're inexpensive, and that is part of the design philosophy.

Wildcat Mesh starter projects — this week

Five concrete jobs, in order, using the gear you have. Each one leaves you with a number written on a piece of tape. By the end you'll know which antenna belongs on the solar repeater and which pigtail belongs in the trash.

PROJECT 1

Build the mesh calibration and save it

gear: H4 · cal kit · your best SMA cable · (u.FL adapter)
  • Sweep 850–1000 MHz, 401 points, IF BW 1 kHz.
  • OPEN / SHORT / LOAD at the cable end → DONE → save slot 0.
  • Repeat with a second cable on CH1 and a THRU → save slot 5.
  • Verify: LOAD on = flat SWR 1.0; OPEN = marker on the Smith rim.
  • Set traces: T1 SWR, T2 SMITH, T3 S21 LOGMAG. Marker 1 tracking minimum. Save again so the setup rides with the slot.

Outcome: from now on the H4 boots ready to measure a 915 antenna in one tap.

PROJECT 2

Grade every pigtail and adapter

gear: slot 5 · every u.FL→SMA pigtail, SMA↔RP-SMA adapter, barrel you own
  • One at a time in the THRU path. Read S21 at 915 MHz. Wiggle test.
  • Label each: 0.4 dB ✓, 1.1 dB meh, BAD.
  • While you're there: the 50 Ω LOAD through each pigtail on S11 — anything worse than −18 dB is a connector problem.

Outcome: a known-good pigtail set. Every later antenna measurement is trustworthy because the lead isn't the variable.

PROJECT 3

Tune (or triage) the Heltec node whips

gear: slot 0 · each Heltec whip · the node itself, powered OFF · tape & pen
  • Screw the whip onto the node's own SMA (or use its pigtail through the known-good adapter from Project 2) with the board unpowered, so the counterpoise is realistic. Hold by the case, arm extended.
  • Record: dip frequency, SWR at 902 / 907 / 915 / 928.
  • Rank them. Best ones → the fixed / solar nodes. If a whip dips at ~868 (very common), it's an EU antenna: trimmable whips get trimmed 1 mm at a time; helical ducks get demoted to "indoor test node."
  • Bonus: measure one whip held in your hand vs. on the desk vs. in the enclosure. Watch how much the environment moves it. That's your intuition for "why does range change when I put it in a box."

Outcome: every node has an antenna you've seen the dip for, with numbers on tape.

PROJECT 4

Verify the E22 module's antenna path

gear: slot 0 · Ebyte E22 module (unpowered!) · its antenna · its pigtail if IPEX version
  • Power off the E22 first. The E22-900 family transmits at up to +30 dBm (1 W) on the high-power variants — a lethal dose for the VNA.
  • Measure the E22's antenna on its own (Project 3 method). Then, if it's an IPEX module, measure its pigtail (Project 2 method). Sum the pigtail loss with the antenna's match to get the real picture.
  • If the E22 is the high-power one, this antenna's match matters more than any other: 3:1 SWR at 1 W reflects a quarter-watt back into the PA. Aim for < 1.5.

Outcome: confidence the high-power node isn't cooking its own amplifier.

PROJECT 5

Sweep the coax runs and TDR one of them

gear: slots 0 & 5 · any coax run to a rooftop / window / solar node · a wide 1–500 MHz cal in slot 2
  • S21 through each run at 915 MHz (both ends accessible) → loss in dB. Compare to the table in the cable-loss card. Decide: keep, shorten, or upgrade to LMR-240/400.
  • Run that can't reach both ends: S11 with the far end open, halve the return loss.
  • Pick the longest one, recall slot 2, TRANSFORM on, VF set, and find its end on the TDR trace. Then put a marker on every bump before the end — those are the connectors and any damage. Measure the length with a tape and compare; adjust VF until they agree and you've characterized the cable.

Outcome: you know where every dB of the feedline budget goes, and you've used TDR on a real cable so it's no longer mysterious.

STRETCH

Build a 915 MHz ground-plane and tune it live

gear: SMA panel jack (female) · 4 × 9 cm stiff wire · 1 × 9 cm radiator · solder
  • Solder the radiator to the center pin, four radials to the flange, bend radials down ~45°.
  • Slot 0 · CW mode at 906.875 MHz · watch SWR live · trim the radiator 1 mm at a time toward ~7.8 cm until the dip lands. Then sweep to confirm the whole band is under 2:1.
  • This will out-perform every whip in the bag and cost $3. Put it on the repeater.

Outcome: your first tuned-from-scratch antenna, and a reference antenna to compare the commercial ones against.

Glossary

Impedance (Z)
How much a load resists AC current, as resistance R plus reactance X: Z = R + jX, in ohms. RF systems are built around 50 Ω. Matching means making the load look like 50 + j0.
Reactance (X)
The "imaginary" part of impedance — energy stored rather than dissipated. Positive X is inductive (coils, long antennas), negative is capacitive (caps, short antennas). Zero at resonance.
Resonance
The frequency where a load's reactance is zero — for an antenna, where it's the "right" electrical length. The bottom of the SWR dip, the Smith trace crossing the horizontal line.
SWR (VSWR)
Standing-wave ratio. 1:1 = no reflection; 2:1 = 11 % of power reflected; 3:1 = 25 %. Radios generally want ≤ 2. Derived from |Γ|: SWR = (1+|Γ|)/(1−|Γ|).
Return loss (RL)
Reflected power in dB: RL = −20·log10|Γ|. Bigger is better (20 dB = only 1 % reflected). The NanoVNA plots it as S11 LOGMAG with a minus sign, so "more negative is better."
Reflection coefficient (Γ, "gamma")
The raw S11 value: what fraction of the wave bounces, with phase. |Γ| = 0 perfect, 1 total reflection. The Smith chart is a plot of Γ.
S-parameters
Sij = signal out of port i when driving port j. S11 = reflection at port 1 (CH0); S21 = transmission from port 1 to port 2 (CH1). The H4 measures those two; it can't measure S12/S22 without physically flipping the device.
Insertion loss
How much signal a device eats when placed in line, in dB: the magnitude of S21. A 3 dB loss = half the power gone.
dB (decibel)
A ratio on a log scale: 10·log10(P2/P1). 3 dB = 2×, 10 dB = 10×, 20 dB = 100×. Negative = loss. Add them up along a signal path.
dBm
Power referenced to 1 milliwatt: 0 dBm = 1 mW, 10 dBm = 10 mW, 20 dBm = 100 mW, 30 dBm = 1 W. Your USB power meter and the SX1262 datasheet both speak dBm.
Calibration plane
The physical point where the OPEN/SHORT/LOAD were connected. The VNA's "zero." Everything between the instrument and that point is mathematically removed; everything after it is measured.
SOLT
Short-Open-Load-Thru — the calibration method the NanoVNA uses. The kit in the box.
Bandwidth (2:1)
The range of frequencies over which an antenna's SWR stays under 2. Fat antennas are wide; thin whips are narrow. A 915 antenna needs ≥ 26 MHz of it to cover 902–928.
Velocity factor (VF)
How fast a signal moves in a cable relative to light in vacuum: 0.66 for solid-polyethylene coax (RG-58, RG-174, RG-316), ~0.82–0.85 for foam. Needed for TDR distance and for cutting phasing lines.
TDR
Time-domain reflectometry: reflections vs. time/distance along a line. The NanoVNA derives it from a frequency sweep via inverse FFT (DISPLAY → TRANSFORM).
Smith chart
A map of all impedances, with 50 Ω in the center, short on the left, open on the right, inductive above, capacitive below. See the Smith card.
Counterpoise / ground plane
The "other half" of a monopole antenna — radials, a metal box, the PCB, or your hand. Tiny mesh nodes have almost none, which is why their whips are touchy.
Common-mode current
RF flowing on the outside of the coax shield, making the feedline part of the antenna. Chokes/baluns stop it. Symptom on the VNA: the SWR changes when you touch the cable.
Q factor
Sharpness of a resonance: center frequency ÷ bandwidth. High-Q = narrow and efficient (traps, crystals); low-Q = broad and forgiving (fat antennas).
Group delay
How long each frequency takes to pass through a device. Flat = distortion-free. Mostly a filter/matching-network thing.
Dynamic range
The gap between the biggest signal the receiver can read and its noise floor. On the H4: ~70 dB below 300 MHz, ~40 dB above 900 MHz. Deep filter notches at 915 MHz will bottom out at the floor.
IF bandwidth
How narrowly the receiver listens at each sweep point. Narrower = quieter but slower. 1 kHz is a fine default; 100–333 Hz for noisy UHF work.
Electrical delay (E-DELAY)
A phase correction that "moves" the reference plane down a cable mathematically. Un-spirals a Smith trace measured through a length of coax without recalibrating.
Touchstone (.s1p / .s2p)
The universal text format for S-parameter data. The NanoVNA writes them to SD; NanoVNA-Saver reads and writes them.
